# Rehoboth Herbal Co. — a note on the product label wording

**Prepared:** 30 August 2026
**For:** Rehoboth Herbal Co.
**This is not legal advice.** It is a flag from your web developer, raised because
the issue affects what we can publish on your website. For anything binding, please
speak to a regulatory consultant.

---

## The short version

Your website will not repeat the health claims that appear on your labels. That
keeps the site itself on the right side of the law.

But the claims are also printed on the packaging, and that is where the real
exposure sits. We wanted to raise it now rather than after launch, because a new
website makes a brand considerably easier to find — including by regulators.

## What the labels currently say

Taken directly from your own label artwork:

| Product | Wording on the label |
|---|---|
| Artemisia Afra | "Assists in supporting the treatment of malaria, hepatitis, and certain cancers" |
| Artemisia Annua A3 | Same, plus "Supports protection against bacterial and viral threats" |
| Moringa | "Assists in protecting liver cells, reducing inflammation and pain, and supporting healthy blood sugar and cholesterol levels" |
| Turmeric | "Helps manage arthritis, cholesterol levels, anxiety, and muscle pain" |
| Rosemary | Headed "MEDICINAL"; "assists in regulating liver function" |
| All five | "IMMUNE BOOSTER CAPSULES" |

## Why it matters

Under the **Medicines and Related Substances Act 101 of 1965**, a product that
claims to treat, prevent or cure a disease is legally a **medicine** — regardless
of what it is made from. Medicines must be registered with **SAHPRA** before they
may be sold. Selling an unregistered medicine is an offence, and SAHPRA can order
a product withdrawn or recalled.

Three points make this more pressing than it might look:

1. **Naming a disease is the trigger.** Malaria, hepatitis and cancer are named
   explicitly. "Assists in supporting the treatment of" does not soften this — the
   disease name is the problem.
2. **Artemisia is actively watched.** It is the plant at the centre of the malaria
   and COVID remedy claims of recent years, and it draws more regulatory attention
   than most.
3. **"Immune booster" is itself a claim.** It appears on all five labels.

There is a secondary commercial cost: Google classifies health content as "Your
Money or Your Life" and actively suppresses sites making unsupported health claims.
Removing them from the website is a search ranking *gain*, not only a risk reduction.

## What we are doing on the website

- No disease names in any product title, description, category, image caption or
  page metadata.
- Ingredients, directions and dosage taken verbatim from your labels — these are
  factual and stay exactly as they are.
- Benefits described as **traditional use** ("traditionally used in South Africa
  to support…") rather than as treatment. This describes heritage, which is
  permitted, instead of efficacy, which is not.
- A disclaimer on every product page stating the products are not intended to
  diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease.

None of this weakens the selling story. Your genuine advantages — grown, dried and
packed on your own farm at Low's Creek, 100% natural, single-ingredient, and
supporting rural farming development through Foundations for Farming — are stronger
and safer than a disease claim, and no competitor can copy them.

## What we would suggest

1. **Have a regulatory consultant review the label wording.** This is the one step
   we would not skip.
2. **Plan a label revision.** Your label artwork is vector, so revised wording drops
   into the existing design without a redesign. We can produce compliant replacement
   copy if that is useful.
3. **Decide before launch**, so the website and the packaging tell the same story.

If registering any product as a complementary medicine is something you want to
pursue, that is a separate and much longer route, and a consultant is the right
first call.
